Project Summary

Governing AI, Governing with AI

Artificial intelligence is no longer just a technical question. It is a political one. Decisions about who builds AI, who controls it, who benefits, and who bears the risk are questions about power, legitimacy, and accountability, which are the core concerns of political science.

This project looks at AI from two directions. The first is governance of AI: the laws, institutions, treaties, corporate policies, and technical standards that shape how AI systems are developed and deployed. Countries are taking very different approaches. The European Union has built a risk-based regulatory framework, the United States has relied on a mix of executive action, state laws, and voluntary industry commitments, and China pairs state-directed development with tight content controls. At the same time, a small number of private companies now make choices with public consequences, which raises old questions about private power in a new form.

The second is governance in an AI world: how AI is reshaping the practice of politics. Governments use algorithms to allocate benefits, police neighborhoods, and screen immigrants. Campaigns use AI to target voters, and synthetic media threatens the shared facts democracy depends on. AI could also strengthen democracy by helping citizens deliberate at scale, making bureaucracies more responsive, and giving people access to legal and civic information.

My Art History minor adds a third lens. Art history has long asked questions about authorship, authenticity, provenance, and representation, the same questions now being raised by generative AI. Who owns an image made by a machine trained on millions of artists' work? How do museums and archives protect cultural heritage when anything can be convincingly faked? How have images been used as political tools, and how does AI change that?

Power

Who holds control over AI: states, corporations, or citizens? How is that power checked?

Legitimacy

When is it acceptable for an algorithm to make decisions that affect people's lives?

Accountability

When AI causes harm, who is responsible, and through what institutions?

Representation

Whose values, languages, and images are built into AI, and whose are left out?

Guiding Questions

  • Can democratic institutions move fast enough to govern a technology that changes every few months?
  • Should AI be governed like a utility, a weapon, a public good, or a product?
  • Does AI concentrate political power, or could it spread it more widely?
  • What would a global governance regime for AI look like, and who would get a seat at the table?
  • How do we protect truth, trust, and cultural memory when images and text can be generated instantly?

Brainstorm

Ideas for the Class

Possible units, debates, and projects for a course on AI and political science. Filter by theme:

01
Institutions & Law

Comparative AI Regulation

Compare how the EU, U.S., China, and other countries regulate AI. Is each approach driven by its political system, its economy, or its values?

Activity: Students each "represent" a country and defend its AI policy in a mock summit.
02
Institutions & Law

Design an AI Agency

Should there be an "FDA for AI"? Look at how regulatory agencies are built, how they get captured by industry, and what powers an AI regulator would need.

Activity: Draft a founding charter for a new federal AI agency, including its mandate, powers, and oversight.
03
Institutions & Law

Corporations as Governments

AI companies write usage policies, content rules, and safety frameworks that act almost like laws. Are tech firms becoming political actors, and how should they be held accountable?

Activity: Analyze a real AI company's usage policy as if it were a constitution.
04
Democracy

Elections in the Age of Deepfakes

Study AI-generated misinformation, microtargeted political ads, and synthetic candidates. What should election law, platforms, and voters do about it?

Activity: Write a proposed state law on AI in political advertising and debate it in class.
05
Democracy

AI for Deliberation

Could AI help citizens deliberate? Look at citizens' assemblies, digital democracy platforms, and tools that summarize public opinion at scale.

Activity: Run a small citizens' assembly on an AI policy question, with and without AI facilitation, and compare.
06
Democracy

The Algorithmic Bureaucracy

Governments increasingly use AI for welfare eligibility, tax audits, and public services. What happens to due process and public trust when the "bureaucrat" is an algorithm?

Activity: Case study of a real public-sector algorithm that failed, and a redesign proposal.
07
Global Politics

The AI Arms Race

Use international relations theory (realism, liberalism, constructivism) to analyze U.S.–China competition, export controls on chips, and autonomous weapons.

Activity: Crisis simulation where teams negotiate an AI arms control agreement.
08
Global Politics

A Global AI Treaty?

Learn from nuclear, climate, and internet governance. Should there be an "IAEA for AI"? What would the Global South gain or lose?

Activity: Draft and vote on a model UN-style resolution on AI safety.
09
Rights & Justice

Algorithmic Bias & Civil Rights

Explore how AI in policing, hiring, housing, and lending can reproduce inequality, and how civil rights law applies (or doesn't).

Activity: Audit a dataset or tool for bias and write a policy memo on the findings.
10
Rights & Justice

Surveillance & Privacy

Facial recognition, predictive policing, and data collection raise questions about liberty and state power. Compare democratic and authoritarian uses of AI surveillance.

Activity: Debate: "Resolved: facial recognition should be banned in public spaces."
11
Rights & Justice

Work, Labor & Political Economy

How will AI change jobs, unions, and inequality? Examine proposals like universal basic income, AI taxes, and worker voice in automation decisions.

Activity: Interview a worker in an industry affected by AI and connect it to course readings.
12
Art & Culture

Authorship & Copyright

Who owns AI-generated art? Connect debates over training data and artists' rights to the history of copying, workshops, and appropriation in art history.

Activity: Compare an AI-generated image to a historical work it imitates and write a short analysis.
13
Art & Culture

Propaganda, Images & Power

From Roman coins to wartime posters, images have always served political power. How does generative AI change political imagery and visual persuasion?

Activity: Curate a mini "exhibition" pairing historical propaganda with AI-generated political images.
14
Art & Culture

Provenance & Cultural Heritage

Art historians trace where objects come from. Apply that to AI: content labeling, watermarking, and protecting museums and archives from forgery and misinformation.

Activity: Visit the Cantor Arts Center and discuss how AI could help or harm how collections are authenticated and displayed.
15
Capstone

Write a Constitution for AI

Bring the whole course together: small groups design a governance structure for AI, covering rights, institutions, checks and balances, and how to amend it.

Final project: Present your AI constitution and defend it against questions from classmates.
